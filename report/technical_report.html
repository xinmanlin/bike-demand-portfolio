Hourly bike-sharing demand: interpretable count models and uncertainty under temporal shift

A study of conditional demand modelling, leakage-safe forecasting and conformal prediction when exchangeability may be violated

Abstract

We benchmark interpretable generalised linear models against tree-based learners on 17,379 observed hours of Capital Bikeshare demand. The study separates an explanatory task (demand conditional on same-hour weather) from a deployable forecasting task (calendar and lagged demand only). A Poisson GLM with dispersion-corrected inference achieves a one-step-ahead test MAE of 41.79 against 168.89 for the global-mean baseline, 66.48 for the best naive persistence baseline (t-168h) and 55.21 for XGBoost using the same historical information. We then compare six conformal interval constructions with the Winkler score; the selected method, Split / Poisson-scaled, reaches 92.8% validation coverage with worst-hour coverage 81.7%, versus 78.0% peak-hour coverage for fixed absolute calibration. Finally we show that residual autocorrelation (lag-1 ACF 0.73) and distribution drift make the exchangeability assumption behind split conformal unlikely to hold, and report coverage as an empirical measurement rather than a guarantee.

1. Data and validation design

The UCI hourly file contains 17,379 observed rows on a 17,544-hour calendar grid, i.e. 165 missing hours. We reindex onto the complete hourly grid before computing lagged features, so a 168-hour lag means exactly the same hour one week earlier rather than the 168th previous record. Splits are aligned to calendar days so a single day never crosses two sets. Period means in Table 1 use all observed hours; the calibration / validation / test means quoted in Section 4 use only modelling rows (those with a complete lag history), so they differ slightly. The raw file contains no zero counts (minimum 1) and 59% of missing hours fall between 02:00 and 05:00, so most missing hours probably represent hours with zero rentals rather than data loss; they are excluded rather than imputed. 36 of the 40 test-period gaps fall on 29-30 October 2012, which coincides with Hurricane Sandy (not verified against operator records).

2. Methods

2.1 Two deliberately separate tasks

Task A (conditional). Poisson/NB GLMs with hour x working-day interactions, season, year, holiday, a quadratic temperature term, humidity, wind and weather category. Task B (deployable, one-step-ahead). The same calendar terms plus log(t-168h demand) and log of the mean demand over hours t-24 to t-1; no contemporaneous weather. All models use calendar variables and historical demand without future-information leakage, but feature representations differ across model families: the trees receive raw lags (including an extra t-24h lag), month and weekday, while the GLM uses log lags and an explicit hour x working-day interaction. The comparison is therefore not a perfectly matched feature-set experiment. Baselines: global mean, hour x working-day mean, and three naive persistence forecasts (t-1h, t-24h and seasonal t-168h).

2.2 Statistical models and inference

Counts are modelled with a log link. Pearson dispersion is 11.56, far above one, so every GLM is fitted with dispersion-corrected (quasi-Poisson) standard errors; the default nonrobust covariance would make the p-values meaningless. The NB dispersion parameter is a plug-in method-of-moments estimate used to define a variance function, not an optimised point predictor. Comparisons and interval selection use validation metrics and three non-overlapping 28-day rolling-origin folds; the frozen test period is never used for selection.

2.3 Prediction intervals

We use split conformal prediction with three nonconformity scales (absolute, sqrt(mu), sqrt(mu + alpha*mu^2)), plus rolling-window calibration, adaptive conformal inference (ACI, Gibbs & Candes 2021) and coarse hour-group (Mondrian) calibration. Rolling and ACI update their calibration pool using only realised counts up to t-1. Methods are ranked by the mean Winkler score at nominal 90%, which penalises both miss-coverage and excessive width; raw average width is not comparable across methods that achieve different coverage.

The conditional Poisson GLM systematically over-predicts on validation (mean bias 31.35 rentals, median actual/predicted 0.890). Adding a single C(yr):mnth interaction (month entered linearly, so the term is identifiable even though training contains 2012 data only for January-May) cuts validation MAE from 53.14 to 47.49: the 2012 growth effect is not constant across the year, which explains much of the systematic bias. Honest reading of the selection. The validation-based selection rule selects Split / Poisson-scaled (validation Winkler 278.7), with ACI essentially tied at 279.1. On the test period, ACI achieves the lowest Winkler score of all six methods (273.5 vs 280.5 for the selected method) while holding 89.6% coverage and 89.8% peak-hour coverage. That is consistent with online calibration being more robust under drift, but two time periods do not establish a significant difference, and the method was not re-selected after seeing the test results. The practical conclusion is narrower and more defensible: a variance-aware score matters a great deal (Winkler 326.7 -> 278.7), while the extra gain from online adaptation is modest on this dataset.

5. Diagnostics: bias and dependence

Task A exposed a structural bias (mean signed error 31.35 rentals). The Task B forecasting GLM shows a much smaller mean signed error (predicted - actual) of 5.09 rentals on validation (median actual/predicted 0.989). Because the two tasks differ in both features and specification, this does not prove that lagged demand alone removed the bias, but it is consistent with recent demand absorbing level shifts that the weather model attributes to fixed year and season effects. Standardised calibration residuals have lag-1 ACF 0.73 and lag-24 ACF 0.01: strong serial dependence that decays quickly with the lag. Peak-hour coverage under fixed absolute calibration is only 78.0%.

Interpretation. Split conformal promises marginal coverage only when calibration and future scores are exchangeable. An exchangeable sequence has the same correlation between every pair of positions, whereas the residual autocorrelation here decays from 0.73 at lag 1 to 0.01 at lag 24, and mean demand drifts between periods. The exchangeability assumption is therefore unlikely to hold, and the coverage numbers above are empirical measurements on the validation and test periods, not finite-sample guarantees. Rolling and adaptive calibration are included precisely to react to this; a production system would additionally need rolling retraining, monitoring of coverage by hour, and weather forecasts rather than observations.

6. Limitations and future work

  • Task A is conditional on observed weather; replacing it with forecasts will degrade accuracy.
  • The exchangeability assumption is unlikely to hold (lag-1 ACF 0.73, mean demand 229.4 -> 281.6 -> 236.9). Coverage is empirical, not guaranteed.
  • The same test period was also used in an early exploratory version of this project; the main conclusions are consistent across the validation period and the three backtest folds.
  • Missing hours (mostly 02:00-05:00, likely zero-rental hours) are excluded rather than imputed as zero, slightly under-representing very low overnight demand.
  • Online calibration assumes a reliable real-time count feed at the end of every hour.
  • Intervals are symmetric and truncated at zero; asymmetric count intervals would be sharper.
  • One city, two years, one public teaching dataset: no claim of novelty or external validity.
  • Tree models use the same historical information with a different feature representation and were not exhaustively tuned; the conclusion is about this setting, not about GLMs dominating trees in general.
